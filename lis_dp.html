1The Problem

Longest Increasing Subsequence (LeetCode #300)

Given a list of numbers, return the length of the longest strictly increasing subsequence.

In plain words: pull a rising chain out of the list, where each number beats the one before it. You may skip numbers. You may not move them.

2What Would I Try First?

We already know: longest rising chain, skipping fine, reordering not.
The obvious idea: walk left to right. Grab each number if it beats the last one you grabbed.

It sounds safe, because everything it grabs really does beat the last one.

It broke at step 2, grabbing the 6. That felt free, and the chain did get longer. But it also raised the bar: now everything later had to beat 6, and the whole rest of the list is smaller. One easy win threw away a bigger one.

The mistake is not where it got stuck. It is back at step 2.

Here is the problem. A choice made early changes what is allowed much later. So we cannot judge a choice by what it gives us right now.
Would it work if we just started somewhere else?
No. Try [1, 100, 2, 3, 4]. It grabs the 1, grabs the 100, then gets stuck and says 2. The real answer is 4. The problem is not where it starts. It is grabbing without checking what that grab costs later.
So next: if grabbing is unsafe, what are we stuck on?

Instead of the huge question ("best chain in the whole list?"), ask a smaller one:

"What is the longest chain that ends right here?"

This is a good thing to be stuck on. The last number is already fixed. The only unknown is what comes just before it, and that must be smaller and earlier. Here that means the 1 or the 2, or nothing at all.

What we do not know: which of those three is longest. To find out we would need to know how long the chain ending at the 1 is, and at the 2. Those are the same question, just smaller.

The question is not "take it or skip it". It is "what do I hook onto?"

Explaining our problem made smaller copies of the same problem. That is the sign recursion will help.

So next: we need someone to answer those smaller questions.

5The Recursion Promise

We already know: to answer about INDEX 4 we need answers about INDEX 2 and 3.

People say recursion is "a function that calls itself". True, but useless. Here is the version you can use:

The promise: hand the function a smaller version of the same question, and it hands back the right answer. You never need to know how it worked it out. You just use the number.
longest_chain_ending_at(current_index = 4)
how long is the chain ending on the 3?
↓  asks: "how long is the chain ending on the 2?"
longest_chain_ending_at(current_index = 3)
↑  hands back LENGTH 2, meaning 1 → 2
longest_chain_ending_at(current_index = 4)
now it can finish

Does this ever stop? Yes. Every call points at a smaller INDEX. 4 asks 3, 3 asks 2, and 2 has nothing smaller to its left so it just answers. The ladder has a bottom.

Does the smaller call need to know who asked it?
No, and that is the point. The call for INDEX 3 answers one fixed question, and the answer is 2 no matter who asked. If a call needed to know its caller, the question would not stand on its own and none of this would work.
So next: pin down exactly what one call gives back.

Hand it over and trust it, up top. Exactly what comes back, underneath.

What does the call for INDEX 2 give back, and why is it not 3?
LENGTH 1. INDEX 2 holds the VALUE 1, and the only earlier numbers are 5 and 6, both too big. So the best chain ending there is just the 1 alone. It is not 3 because we have walked past three numbers, and walking past is not using. We count links in the chain, not steps along the list.
So next: follow one call down, and watch the answer come back.

7Down to the Bottom, and Back Up

We already know: one call gives back how long the chain ending at one spot is.

No big tree. One path, all the way down, then all the way back.

The bottom answers 1, not 0. A chain of one number is still a chain. Saying 0 would mean no chain ends here at all, which is false. Every number is a chain of 1, and that holds up everything else.

Going down we asked. Coming up we use. At INDEX 3, after getting LENGTH 1 back, then at INDEX 4 after getting LENGTH 2 back:

Follow the 3 at the top. It is 1 + 2. That 2 came from below, and it was 1 + 1, where the inner 1 came from the bottom. So the final 3 is really the bottom's answer, changed twice on the way up.

At INDEX 3 we wrote 1 + 1. There is a 2 sitting right there. Why not 1 + 2?
That 2 is a VALUE, and VALUEs never go into this sum. The first 1 is one number, mine, joining the chain. The second is a count that came back from below. We count links, we do not add up the numbers.
So next: INDEX 3 was only one option. What were the rest?

8Every Choice We Have

We already know: hooking onto INDEX 3 gave a chain of LENGTH 3.

At INDEX 4 we check every earlier spot, plus hooking onto nothing. Not the nearest one. All of them. That is exactly what greedy refused to do.

Two are too big. Two work, giving LENGTH 2 and 3. Starting alone always works and gives LENGTH 1.

Why nothing is missing. Take any chain ending on our number. Either something sits right before it, and that thing is at some earlier smaller spot which we checked, or nothing does, and the chain is our number alone. There is no third kind.

What if we forgot the "start alone" option and ran it on [9, 8, 7]?
We would get 0, which is wrong. Nothing has a smaller number to its left, so no option is ever allowed and there is nothing to compare. The right answer is 1, because any single number is a chain. This is why starting at 1 instead of 0 is not a detail. It is the bottom case in disguise.
So next: what does one call actually need to remember?

9The Bookmark

We already know: a call checks every earlier spot, plus starting alone.

Say we stop halfway and hand the job to a friend, leaving one note. What has to be on it so they can carry on without asking us anything?

That is what people call a "state". The smallest note that fully describes the question left to answer.

Before the spot: things to hook onto. At it: where our chain ends. After it: invisible.

One thing to notice, because it matters later: the note always moves left, never right. That will tell us which way to fill the table.

So next: three options gave 2, 3 and 1. How do we squash them into one?

10Where max and +1 Come From

We already know: the allowed options gave LENGTH 2, 3 and 1.

Three numbers, one needed. Nothing in the code decides this. The wording of the question does.

Why max. All three built real chains, and all are legal. The question asked for the longest, so keep the biggest. Change the wording and the operation changes with it:

If the question saysUse
longest, biggest, mostmax
shortest, cheapest, leastmin
how many ways+
is it possible at allor

Why +1. What did this option do right now, before asking anyone anything? It put exactly one number on the end of a chain that already existed. One number joined, so the count goes up by one.

max comes from "longest". The +1 comes from adding exactly one number.

The +1 counts a number. It is never the number itself. At INDEX 4 the VALUE is 3, so +3 feels natural. But we count how many numbers are in the chain, not their total. On [1, 9] the answer is 2, not 10.
So next: write it down as a rule.

11The Rule

Now we can write the rule, because it says nothing new.

longest_chain_ending_at(current_index) = the biggest of:

    1                                      # start alone

    1 + longest_chain_ending_at(previous_index)
        for every earlier spot whose value is smaller

answer = the biggest of those, across every spot
That last line is not decoration. Each call only knows about chains ending at one spot. Nobody said where the best chain ends, so we ask everywhere and keep the biggest. Forgetting it is the most common way to get this almost right.

13The Code, Line by Line

We already know: every line below matches something we worked out by hand.
# Gives back how many numbers are in the longest rising
# chain that ends on numbers[current_index].
def longest_chain_ending_at(current_index):
    longest_so_far = 1
    for previous_index in range(current_index):
        if numbers[previous_index] < numbers[current_index]:
            longest_so_far = max(longest_so_far,
                                 1 + longest_chain_ending_at(previous_index))   # RECURSION HAPPENS HERE
    return longest_so_far


answer = max(longest_chain_ending_at(current_index)
             for current_index in range(len(numbers)))
def longest_chain_ending_at(current_index): The note becomes the input. One spot in, one count out.
longest_so_far = 1 "Start alone" and the bottom case at once. If no earlier spot works, the loop does nothing and this 1 comes back. Delete it and [9, 8, 7] gives 0.
for previous_index in range(current_index): Check every earlier spot, not just the nearest. This is the part greedy skipped.
if numbers[previous_index] < numbers[current_index]: Only a smaller number may sit before ours. Use <= and [2, 2, 2] starts saying 3. The loop walks over spots, this line compares values, and we need both.
longest_so_far = max(longest_so_far, 1 + longest_chain_ending_at(previous_index)) The inner call hands back a count. The 1 + pays for our own number. The max keeps this option only if it beats what we had. The VALUE never enters the sum.
return longest_so_far The promise, kept. We started at 1 and only swapped in something longer that still ends here.
answer = max(... for every spot) Outside the function. The chain ends somewhere and nobody said where, so ask everywhere. Not optional, and not the last spot.
Say the recursive line out loud "Take how long the chain ending back there was, add one for my own number, and keep it only if it beats my best so far."

Six lines, six ideas, all worked out before they were written.

Which one line stops this running forever?
The orange one. The earlier spot is always smaller than the current one, so every call moves left, and spots cannot go below 0. If it looped over the whole list instead, a call could ask itself and never finish. The if does not save us, because it compares values, not direction.
So next: run it on the whole list.

15What Gets Done Twice?

We already know: the answer is the biggest across every spot.

While working out INDEX 4, two routes end in the same place. Route A hooks straight onto INDEX 2. Route B hooks onto INDEX 3, which hooks onto INDEX 2. Both finish by asking the exact same thing.

Two different paths, one identical question.

Be clear about what is wasted. It is not that the number 1 shows up twice. It is that the program works out the same answer again from scratch, checking the same spots and redoing the same comparisons.

That is just inside one branch. Across the whole run, INDEX 2 gets asked four times, and on a longer list this gets much worse fast.

On the list 1 to 20: only 20 different questions, but 1,048,575 calls. That gap is why the next step exists.
So next: the fix is obvious once you see the waste.

16Write It Down

We already know: the same spot was worked out more than once.

The fix is one sentence: before working something out, check if we already did. We look things up by the note, which is the spot. We store what the call gave back, which is a count.

solved_lengths = {}

def longest_chain_ending_at(current_index):
    if current_index in solved_lengths:
        return solved_lengths[current_index]

    longest_so_far = 1
    for previous_index in range(current_index):
        if numbers[previous_index] < numbers[current_index]:
            longest_so_far = max(longest_so_far,
                                 1 + longest_chain_ending_at(previous_index))

    solved_lengths[current_index] = longest_so_far
    return longest_so_far


answer = max(longest_chain_ending_at(current_index)
             for current_index in range(len(numbers)))

Three new lines. Make an empty box, check it first, and fill it before handing the answer back.

Say one entry out loud solved_lengths[2] = 1 means "the longest chain ending on the 1 at spot 2 has one number in it". That is the promise, word for word.

Look it up by the spot. Store what the call would have given back.

Nothing else changed. Same promise, same options, same +1, same max, same answers. Only the repeated work is gone.

Why look things up by the spot alone, with nothing about the chain so far?
Because the chain so far cannot change the answer. "How long is the chain ending at spot 2" is 1 no matter who asked. We tested that idea in section 9 and dropped it. If the answer did depend on who asked, the spot alone would not describe the question and this would give wrong answers.
So next: if every spot gets worked out once, why call at all?

17A Call and a Box Are the Same Thing

We already know: each spot gets worked out once and stored.

Look at what we built: one slot per spot, each holding a count. That is a list. And a list you look things up in is a table. So we keep the name, and only the brackets change:

longest_chain_ending_at(2) becomes longest_chain_ending_at[2]

Round brackets ask for it. Square brackets look it up.

A call, a stored entry and a table box are three places to keep one fact.

The only difference is when the answers get made. A call asks the moment it needs one. A table fills boxes in an order where whatever you need is already there, so you never ask, you only read.

Which order? Section 9 told us: the note always moves left. So fill left to right, and everything we need is behind us.

So next: fill it in and read every box out loud.

def length_of_lis(numbers):
    if not numbers:
        return 0

    # one box per spot, all starting at 1
    longest_chain_ending_at = [1] * len(numbers)

    for current_index in range(len(numbers)):
        for previous_index in range(current_index):
            if numbers[previous_index] < numbers[current_index]:
                longest_chain_ending_at[current_index] = max(
                    longest_chain_ending_at[current_index],
                    1 + longest_chain_ending_at[previous_index])

    return max(longest_chain_ending_at)

No recursion, no lookups. Same five answers, made in an order that guarantees they are ready when needed.

Could we fill it right to left instead?
Not this version. Each box reads boxes to its left, so going right to left would read boxes still holding 1. Box 4 would work out 1 + 1 = 2 and we would answer 2 instead of 3. You can build a right to left version, but only by changing the question to "longest chain starting here", which flips everything. Fill order follows from which way the note moves.
So next: can we throw any boxes away?

19Can We Use Less Memory?

We already know: each box reads boxes to its left.

Usually the next trick is to notice only the last couple of boxes get read, and throw the rest away. Does that work here?

ProblemA box readsThrow boxes away?
Climbing Stairs, House Robberonly the last twoyes, keep 2
This problempossibly every earlier boxno, keep all

Our inner loop checks every earlier spot, with no limit on how far back a match sits. In [1, 100, 101, 102, 2] the last box's only match is the very first box. Throw it away and the answer is gone.

So the honest answer is: we cannot. Not every problem shrinks, and knowing why beats forcing a trick that does not exist here. There is a faster version using a different idea entirely, but that is a separate story.
So next: the traps.

21Remembering This Later

The clue to watch for

When a problem says "keep the order, skipping is fine, find the longest", ask "how good is the answer that ends right here?" at every spot, then take the biggest.

Anchoring on where something ends is the reusable trick. It turns a vague question into one with a short, checkable list of options. The same trick works on longest chain of pairs, Russian doll envelopes, and longest arithmetic subsequence.
