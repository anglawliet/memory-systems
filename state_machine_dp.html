The Problem

Best Time to Buy and Sell Stock with Cooldown (LeetCode #309)

You have stock prices for each day. You can buy, sell, or rest. After selling, you must wait one day (cooldown) before buying again. Maximize profit.

prices = [1, 2, 3, 0, 2]

Best strategy: buy day 0 ($1), sell day 1 ($2), cooldown day 2, buy day 3 ($0), sell day 4 ($2). Profit = (2-1) + (2-0) = $3.

0 Is This a DP Problem?

What is the problem asking me to compute?

Maximum total profit from buying and selling stocks, with a cooldown constraint after each sale.

Why doesn't greedy work?

Greedy idea: "Buy at the lowest, sell at the highest."

Why it fails: The cooldown constraint creates dependencies between transactions. Selling on day 1 means I cannot buy on day 2. So a locally optimal sell might prevent a globally optimal buy. I can't decide each day independently because today's action constrains tomorrow's options.

Why would brute force create overlapping subproblems?

Different sequences of buy/sell decisions can lead to the same situation (same day, same state). Example: "buy day 0, sell day 1, cooldown day 2" and "wait day 0, wait day 1, wait day 2" both arrive at day 3 in the REST state. Same remaining problem.

Which family?

State Machine DP. My available actions depend on what state I'm in. The constraint (cooldown) creates distinct states with different legal moves.

1 The Recursive Question

State it entirely in English before introducing variables:

"What is the maximum profit I can earn from day X onward, given my current situation?"

The function answers one question: from this day forward, in this situation, what's the best I can do?

But what is "my current situation"? That's what I need to figure out next. I can't write the function signature until I know what state information matters.

2 Discover the Base Cases

"If I keep making decisions day after day, when must I eventually stop?"
STOP: Past the last day (day == len(prices))
What situation have I reached? There are no more trading days. The market is closed.
Why can't I continue? No prices left. Nothing to buy or sell.
Why is the remaining problem trivial? Zero days of trading means zero additional profit, regardless of my state.
What should this return? 0.
Why 0? No more trades can happen. Whether I'm holding, resting, or in cooldown, there's nothing left to do. No future profit.
Is this the only stop? Yes. Every day before the last has at least one legal action (even if it's just waiting). The only time I have zero actions is when there are no days left.
Note: if I'm holding a stock when time runs out, it's unsold. That's a loss, not an error. The function returns 0 additional profit from this point. The cost of the unsold stock was already accounted for when I bought it.

3 Discover the Choices

What is my current situation?

It's a particular day. I see today's stock price. I need to decide what to do today.

prices = [1, 2, 3, 0, 2]
I'm standing on some day, looking at today's price.

What am I allowed to do?

The problem says three actions: buy, sell, or do nothing.

What am I forbidden from doing?

THE KEY QUESTION: "Can I always do all three?"
Can I always buy? No. I can't buy if I already own a stock (hold at most one at a time).
Can I always sell? No. I can't sell if I don't own a stock.
Can I always do nothing? Yes. Waiting is always legal.
Is there an additional constraint? Yes: after selling, I must wait one day before buying again. So even if I don't own a stock, I might not be allowed to buy yet.

Why are these the only legal actions?

The problem defines exactly three actions (buy, sell, rest). But their availability depends on my current situation. That's different from knapsack where "grab or skip" is always available. Here, I need to know what situation I'm in before I can figure out my choices.

This means I must first discover the situations.

What situations can I be in?

Let me list every possible situation and ask: what's different about what I'm allowed to do?

Situation A: I don't own a stock, and I didn't sell yesterday.
→ I'm free. I can buy or wait. Two legal actions.
Situation B: I own a stock.
→ I'm holding. I can sell or keep holding. Two legal actions.
Situation C: I don't own a stock, but I sold yesterday.
→ I'm in cooldown. The rule says I must wait. Zero choices. I can only wait.
How do I know these are the only three? The only constraint is "wait one day after selling." That creates exactly one special situation (just sold, must wait). Everything else is either "I own a stock" or "I'm free to act." Three situations. Three states. No more.

These three situations are the states:

REST
I don't own anything. I'm free to act.
HOLD
I own a stock. Waiting for the right time to sell.
SOLD
I just sold. Mandatory cooldown. Can't do anything today.

4 Explain Each Choice

Now I can derive the choices for each situation. In each state, I ask: "what actions are legal right now?"

FROM REST: "I'm free. What can I do?"
BUY
What am I doing? Spending money to buy a stock at today's price.
Why is this legal? I don't own anything and I'm not in cooldown. Buying is allowed.
What changes immediately? I lose prices[day] in cash. I now own a stock.
What stays unchanged? Everything before today. Past profit is locked in.
What remains to solve? From tomorrow onward, maximize profit while holding. -prices[day] + max_profit(day+1, "hold")
WAIT
What am I doing? Nothing. Skip this day.
Why is this legal? Waiting is always legal.
What changes immediately? Nothing. No money spent, no stock acquired.
What stays unchanged? Everything. My state doesn't change.
What remains to solve? Same problem starting tomorrow. max_profit(day+1, "rest")
Are there other options? Can I sell? No, I don't own anything. Buy or wait are the only legal actions. Decision: max(buy, wait).
FROM HOLD: "I own a stock. What can I do?"
SELL
What am I doing? Selling my stock at today's price.
Why is this legal? I own a stock. Selling is allowed.
What changes immediately? I gain prices[day] in cash. I no longer own a stock.
What stays unchanged? Past transactions. Their profit is locked in.
What remains to solve? Tomorrow I'm in SOLD (not REST!). Cooldown forces one day wait. prices[day] + max_profit(day+1, "sold")
KEEP HOLDING
What am I doing? Not selling. Waiting for a better price.
Why is this legal? Holding is always legal.
What changes immediately? Nothing. Still own the stock.
What stays unchanged? Everything.
What remains to solve? max_profit(day+1, "hold")
Are there other options? Can I buy another stock? No, hold at most one. Sell or keep are the only legal actions. Decision: max(sell, keep).
FROM SOLD: "I just sold. What can I do?"
What are my legal actions? None. The cooldown rule says I must wait one day after selling. Buying is forbidden. Selling is impossible (I don't own anything). Waiting is the only option.
What changes? Day advances. Cooldown is served.
What new situation does this create? Tomorrow I'll be in REST (free to act again).
What remains to solve? max_profit(day+1, "rest")
Why does the SOLD state even exist? Without it, after selling I'd go straight to REST and could buy again immediately. The SOLD state is how the code enforces the one-day gap. The constraint created this state.

5 Discover the State

What information must I know to uniquely describe the remaining problem?

Candidate: day (which day am I on?)
Why do I need it? Different days have different prices. "What's the max profit from day 2 onward?" is a completely different question from "from day 4 onward?"
What if I remove it? "What's the max profit from some day onward?" is ambiguous. I can't determine the answer without knowing which day.
Candidate: current state (rest, hold, or sold)
Why do I need it? Different states have different legal actions. "Max profit from day 3 in REST" lets me buy. "Max profit from day 3 in SOLD" forces me to wait. Different answers.
What if I remove it? "Max profit from day 3 in ??? state" is ambiguous. I can't determine which actions are legal.
Candidate: which stock I bought / at what price
Do I need it? No. The buy price was already subtracted as a cost when I chose to buy. The remaining problem only cares about "am I holding or not?" and "which day is it?" The exact purchase price doesn't affect future decisions.
Verdict: Not part of the state.
State = (day, situation)
Two variables: which day (0 to n-1), and which of three states (rest, hold, sold). At most 3n unique subproblems.

6 State Invariant

max_profit(day, state)
"I promise everything before this day has already been handled. Past transactions are locked in. I only care about maximizing profit from day onward, given my current state."

Does every recursive call preserve this?

Every call moves to day+1. It never revisits a previous day. It never undoes a past transaction. The state (rest/hold/sold) fully captures the consequence of past actions without storing the full history.

Verification: When I call max_profit(day+1, "hold"), I've already committed to buying on the current day. The buy cost is accounted for. The child call only needs to handle day+1 onward in the HOLD state. Invariant preserved.

7 Derive the Transition

State: max_profit(day, state). For each transition, justify the cost, the state change, and why.

FROM REST: Two choices
BUY (rest → hold)
Action: Buy stock at today's price.
Immediate cost: -prices[day]. Money spent.
State change: day → day+1 (tomorrow), state: rest → hold (now own a stock).
Why state changes: I just acquired a stock. Tomorrow I'm holding it.
Call: -prices[day] + max_profit(day+1, "hold")
WAIT (rest → rest)
Action: Do nothing.
Immediate cost: +0. No transaction.
State change: day → day+1, state stays rest.
Why state stays: Nothing happened. Still free tomorrow.
Call: max_profit(day+1, "rest")
Decision: max(buy, wait).
FROM HOLD: Two choices
SELL (hold → sold)
Action: Sell stock at today's price.
Immediate reward: +prices[day]. Money received.
State change: day → day+1, state: hold → sold (must cooldown next).
Why sold, not rest? The cooldown rule. After selling, I must wait one day. SOLD enforces that gap.
Call: prices[day] + max_profit(day+1, "sold")
KEEP HOLDING (hold → hold)
Action: Don't sell. Wait for a better price.
Immediate cost: +0. No transaction.
State change: day → day+1, state stays hold.
Why state stays: Still own the same stock.
Call: max_profit(day+1, "hold")
Decision: max(sell, keep).
FROM SOLD: No choice (mandatory cooldown)
Action: Wait. No other option.
Immediate cost: +0. Just waiting.
State change: day → day+1, state: sold → rest (cooldown complete).
Why rest? The one-day gap is served. Tomorrow I'm free to act again.
Call: max_profit(day+1, "rest")

8 Derive the Recurrence

Now the recurrence writes itself:

max_profit(day, state) =
    0                                               if day == len(prices)

    max(-prices[day] + max_profit(day+1, "hold"),
        max_profit(day+1, "rest"))                  if state == "rest"

    max(prices[day] + max_profit(day+1, "sold"),
        max_profit(day+1, "hold"))                  if state == "hold"

    max_profit(day+1, "rest")                       if state == "sold"

The code

def max_profit(prices, day, state, cache):
    if day == len(prices):
        return 0
    if (day, state) in cache:
        return cache[(day, state)]

    if state == "rest":
        buy = -prices[day] + max_profit(prices, day + 1, "hold", cache)
        wait = max_profit(prices, day + 1, "rest", cache)
        result = max(buy, wait)
    elif state == "hold":
        sell = prices[day] + max_profit(prices, day + 1, "sold", cache)
        keep = max_profit(prices, day + 1, "hold", cache)
        result = max(sell, keep)
    else:
        result = max_profit(prices, day + 1, "rest", cache)

    cache[(day, state)] = result
    return result

max_profit(prices, 0, "rest", {})
REST
max(buy, wait). Buy costs money now but opens future sells.
HOLD
max(sell, keep). Sell for today's price or hold for a better one.
SOLD
No choice. Must cooldown. Go to REST.

9 Memoization

Why does repeated work occur?

Different trading paths reach the same (day, state):

max_profit(day=0, rest)
  buy: max_profit(day=1, hold)
    sell: max_profit(day=2, sold)
      cooldown: max_profit(day=3, rest)  ─ ─ ─ ┐
    keep: max_profit(day=2, hold)               │
      sell: max_profit(day=3, sold)             │ SAME!
        cooldown: max_profit(day=4, rest)       │
      keep: max_profit(day=3, hold)             │
  wait: max_profit(day=1, rest)                 │
    buy: max_profit(day=2, hold)                │
      sell: max_profit(day=3, sold)             │
        cooldown: max_profit(day=4, rest) ─ ─ ─ ┘

Which states repeat?

(day=3, rest) appears from multiple trading histories. "Buy day 0, sell day 1, cooldown day 2" and "wait day 0, wait day 1, wait day 2" both arrive at day 3 in REST. The future is identical regardless of how I got here.

How memoization removes repetition

3 states x n days

10 Tabulation

Why the table dimensions come from the state

State = (day, situation). Day ranges from 0 to n-1. Situation is one of three values. But each day only depends on the previous day, so I just need three variables, not a full table.

Why the iteration order works

Every transition moves to day+1. If I fill from day 0 forward, every dependency (the next day's values) would need to be computed first. But I'm computing profit from day X onward, so I fill backwards from the last day (or I use the tabulation trick: track three running variables forward, where each day's values only depend on the previous day's values).

11 Space Optimization

Which previous states are actually needed?

Each day only depends on the previous day's three values (hold, sold, rest). I don't need any day before that.

Why older states can be discarded

Three variables are enough. No array needed. O(1) space.

def max_profit(prices):
    if not prices:
        return 0

    hold = -prices[0]
    sold = 0
    rest = 0

    for day in range(1, len(prices)):
        new_hold = max(hold, rest - prices[day])
        new_sold = hold + prices[day]
        new_rest = max(rest, sold)
        hold, sold, rest = new_hold, new_sold, new_rest

    return max(sold, rest)

12 Pattern Recognition

What clues suggested this DP pattern?

"Available actions depend on my situation" → Not a simple grab/skip. I need to track which state I'm in.
"After selling, must wait one day" → A constraint on consecutive actions. This creates an extra state (SOLD) beyond the obvious two (have stock / don't have stock).
"Maximize profit" → Optimization over choices → DP.

What should I immediately think when I see a similar problem?

"Constraints restrict my actions based on recent history. Each distinct situation is a state. Each legal action is a transition." For any problem where today's options depend on what I did recently, think state machine DP.

What generalizes to future problems?

States come from constraints. More constraints = more states.
Without the cooldown, only 2 states needed (hold/rest). The constraint added the third.
Each state has its own set of legal actions. Derive them, don't memorize them.
Space optimization: if each step only depends on the previous step, three variables are enough.

13 Common Mistakes

Wrong state: forgetting the SOLD state
Using only HOLD and REST. After selling, going directly to REST allows buying on the next day, violating the cooldown rule.
How to detect: test with prices=[1,2,3,0,2]. If your answer is higher than 3, the cooldown isn't being enforced.
Wrong transition: sell goes to REST instead of SOLD
Writing max_profit(day+1, "rest") after selling instead of max_profit(day+1, "sold"). Same bug as above, just in the transition instead of the state design.
How to detect: trace by hand. After selling on day 1, can you buy on day 2? If yes, transition is wrong.
Wrong initialization: hold = 0 instead of -prices[0]
If hold starts at 0, the algorithm thinks holding costs nothing. On day 0, buying means spending prices[0], so hold must start as -prices[0].
How to detect: if your profit is too high, check whether the buy cost is being subtracted.
Wrong final answer: returning max(hold, sold, rest)
Including HOLD in the final answer. Ending in HOLD means you're stuck with an unsold stock, which is never optimal (you paid for it but never got the money back). Return max(sold, rest).
How to detect: test with prices=[5]. Greedy would say hold = -5 (bought but never sold). If your answer is 0 (correctly: do nothing), this is fine. If it's -5, you're counting hold.

1 Longest Increasing Subsequence

▶
LEETCODE #300
Given an array of integers, find the length of the longest strictly increasing subsequence.
Example:
[10, 9, 2, 5, 3, 7, 101, 18]

Some increasing subsequences:
[2, 5, 7, 101] = length 4
[2, 5, 7, 18] = length 4
[2, 3, 7, 101] = length 4

Answer: 4. No increasing subsequence of length 5 exists.
A subsequence keeps the original order but can skip elements. It doesn't have to be contiguous.
CLASSIFICATION
Recursive question: "What is the longest increasing subsequence ending at index i?"
State: lis_ending_at(index). Just one variable: which element I'm ending at.
Why not start/end like palindrome? A subsequence can skip elements. The state is just where the chain currently ends.
CHOICES (DISCOVERED, NOT LISTED)
Situation: I'm at element nums[index]. I want to build the longest chain ending here.
What can I do? Look at every previous element. If nums[prev] < nums[index], I could extend that chain by appending my element.
Why check ALL prev? Any smaller previous element could end the longest chain. I don't know which without trying all.
If no valid prev exists? Start a new chain of length 1 (just this element alone).

Transitions

EXTEND: nums[prev] < nums[index]
Action: Extend the chain ending at prev by appending nums[index].
Immediate reward: +1. One more element in the chain.
Why can we extend? nums[prev] < nums[index]. The sequence stays strictly increasing.
Call: 1 + lis_ending_at(prev)
STANDALONE: no valid prev
lis_ending_at(index) = 1. This element alone is an increasing subsequence of length 1.
Decision: best = max(1, max(1 + lis_ending_at(prev) for valid prev))

Recursion

def lis_ending_at(nums, index, cache):
    if index in cache:
        return cache[index]

    best = 1
    for prev in range(index):
        if nums[prev] < nums[index]:
            best = max(best, 1 + lis_ending_at(nums, prev, cache))

    cache[index] = best
    return best

def longest_increasing(nums):
    cache = {}
    return max(lis_ending_at(nums, i, cache) for i in range(len(nums)))

Tabulation

def longest_increasing(nums):
    n = len(nums)
    lis = [1] * n

    for i in range(1, n):
        for prev in range(i):
            if nums[prev] < nums[i]:
                lis[i] = max(lis[i], lis[prev] + 1)

    return max(lis)
Not a typical state machine, but each element's "state" is the length of the best subsequence ending there. The transition: can I extend any earlier subsequence? O(n²) with DP, O(n log n) with binary search (patience sorting).

2 Burst Balloons

▶
LEETCODE #312
Given n balloons with values, burst them one at a time. When you burst balloon i, you earn nums[i-1] * nums[i] * nums[i+1] (product of it and its current neighbors). Maximize total coins. Boundaries are 1.
Example:
nums = [3, 1, 5, 8] → with boundaries: [1, 3, 1, 5, 8, 1]

One possible order:
Burst 1 (neighbors 3, 5): 3×1×5 = 15. Remaining: [3, 5, 8]
Burst 5 (neighbors 3, 8): 3×5×8 = 120. Remaining: [3, 8]
Burst 3 (neighbors 1, 8): 1×3×8 = 24. Remaining: [8]
Burst 8 (neighbors 1, 1): 1×8×1 = 8. Remaining: []

Total: 15 + 120 + 24 + 8 = 167 (this happens to be optimal).
The tricky part: when you burst a balloon, its neighbors change. Bursting [1] makes [3] and [5] become neighbors.
CLASSIFICATION
Why is this hard? Bursting a balloon changes the neighbors of adjacent balloons. Subproblems depend on each other.
Key insight: Think about which balloon to burst LAST, not first. If balloon k is the last to burst in range [left, right], everything else is already gone, so its neighbors are the fixed boundaries left-1 and right+1.
Family: Interval DP. State = (left, right) defining a range.
CHOICES (DISCOVERED, NOT LISTED)
Situation: I have balloons in range [left, right]. I need to burst them all and maximize coins.
Why not think "first to burst"? If I burst k first, its neighbors shift, making the remaining subproblem dependent on that choice. Subproblems aren't independent.
Why "last to burst" works: If k is the last in [left, right], everything else is already gone. k's neighbors are the boundaries: nums[left-1] and nums[right+1]. These are fixed, not shifted. The left and right subproblems are independent.
Choices: Try every balloon k in [left, right] as the last one burst. Take the maximum.

Transitions

For each k in [left, right]: Burst k LAST
Action: Choose balloon k as the last one burst in range [left, right].
Immediate reward: nums[left-1] * nums[k] * nums[right+1]. When k is the last balloon, its neighbors are the range boundaries.
Left subproblem: max_coins(left, k-1). All balloons to the left of k, burst before k.
Right subproblem: max_coins(k+1, right). All balloons to the right of k, burst before k.
Call: nums[left-1] * nums[k] * nums[right+1] + max_coins(left, k-1) + max_coins(k+1, right)
Base case: left > right
No balloons in this range. Return 0.

Recursion

def max_coins(nums, left, right, cache):
    if left > right:
        return 0
    if (left, right) in cache:
        return cache[(left, right)]

    best = 0
    for last in range(left, right + 1):
        coins = nums[left - 1] * nums[last] * nums[right + 1]
        coins += max_coins(nums, left, last - 1, cache)
        coins += max_coins(nums, last + 1, right, cache)
        best = max(best, coins)

    cache[(left, right)] = best
    return best

def burst_balloons(nums):
    nums = [1] + nums + [1]
    return max_coins(nums, 1, len(nums) - 2, {})

Tabulation

def burst_balloons(nums):
    nums = [1] + nums + [1]
    n = len(nums)
    table = [[0] * n for _ in range(n)]

    for length in range(1, n - 1):
        for left in range(1, n - length):
            right = left + length - 1
            for last in range(left, right + 1):
                coins = nums[left - 1] * nums[last] * nums[right + 1]
                coins += table[left][last - 1]
                coins += table[last + 1][right]
                table[left][right] = max(table[left][right], coins)

    return table[1][n - 2]
Interval DP: table[left][right] = max coins from bursting all balloons in [left, right]. Fill by increasing interval length (like palindrome DP). The twist: "burst last" reversal makes subproblems independent.
